# Opening ideation method

Mathieu facilitates; Diana and Damiens contribute equally. This method goes from raw ideas to two or three clear concepts, then selects **one concept as the basis for the proposed future KBC product**. After that, choose a small proof of concept in [section 4 of the day-of guide](WORKFLOW_TEMPLATE.md#4-select-the-four-hour-proof-within-that-product-concept).

**Opening target:** **00:00–00:10 — Understand** the brief and agree on the user problem together. **00:10–00:40 — Mathieu facilitates** silent ideas, open brainstorming, grouping, concept formation, selection of the proposed KBC product, and its first small proof. Divide those 30 minutes according to how the discussion develops; move on when an exit check is met. **From about 00:40 — Diana and Damiens define the immediate interface** for that proof, then coding starts as soon as the first request and result are agreed. The 40-minute handoff is a team target, not a researched optimal duration; adjust if a material rule in the brief needs clarification.

## Materials to set up before the brief

- **Each person:** a pen and paper for their own problem statement; a small stack of post-its for ideas. Use one post-it per idea. Plain paper cut into slips works too.
- **On the table or wall:** the exact brief, a visible countdown timer, and three labelled areas: **All ideas**, **Shortlist**, **Chosen direction**.
- **For the record:** keep [WORKFLOW_TEMPLATE.md](WORKFLOW_TEMPLATE.md) open. Mathieu records the agreed problem, shortlisted ideas, and decision there. A photo of the post-it wall preserves the full brainstorm.

## Agree on the problem

1. Read the exact brief and judging criteria individually. Each person notes the user, current pain, required outcome, available data, constraints, and any mandatory trust or approval rule.
2. Before discussing, **each person writes their own one-sentence problem**: **“[User] needs to [outcome] because [specific current pain].”** This sentence belongs to the problem step; do not repeat it on every idea post-it.
3. Share the three statements in turn. Compare differences in user, pain, and outcome against the brief. Agree on one shared statement; Mathieu records it in [section 1 of the day-of guide](WORKFLOW_TEMPLATE.md#1-capture-and-define-the-problem), marking supporting points as brief facts, evidence, or assumptions. Do not discard a disagreement until it is understood.
4. Ask an organizer about a material unknown if possible. If it remains unanswered, write the assumption; do not silently turn it into a requirement.

**Exit check:** Everyone can explain the same user problem and mandatory constraints in their own words. Move on as soon as they can; resolve a material disagreement before choosing a solution.

## Write ideas independently

Work silently for a few minutes so the first speaker does not set the direction. Write **one word or a short phrase per post-it**, as many as you can. Because the user and pain are already agreed, the note only needs to remind you of a possible direction. A vague spark such as “Kate 3.0 + biometrics” is welcome alongside clearer notes such as “compare options” or “spot missing document.” Do not stop to complete a sentence or fill in a template. Use the [innovation lenses](IDEAS.md#ten-innovation-lenses) only if ideas stall.

## Brainstorm together

**Rule for this phase: add ideas, including vague ones; do not criticize, reject, rank, or defend them yet.** Put the post-its in **All ideas** and read them quickly. Explain a note only if someone cannot understand it; one short sentence is enough. Add new post-its whenever another idea occurs, including ideas sparked by someone else's note. There is no limit on the number of ideas. Mathieu keeps the turns moving so every person contributes. Questions about feasibility, value, or fit wait until after the brainstorm.

## Organize the raw ideas

Group post-its that mean the same thing; keep different angles visible even when they address the same problem. Give each group a short label. Remove only exact duplicates from the wall, and keep a photo of all the raw notes. This is still organization, not a verdict on which idea wins.

## Turn ideas into two or three concepts

**All three work at the wall.** Move post-its, add new ones, combine complementary ideas, and split ideas that hide different approaches. The end of the open brainstorm means evaluation can begin; it does not forbid a new idea or a better combination.

1. **Open preferences:** Each person points to a first choice and a backup, then briefly defends their first choice: *What could it change for the agreed user?* The others comment, question, improve it, or propose a combination. Challenge and refinement are welcome now; save detailed feasibility checks for the next step.
2. **Provisional ranking:** Together, order the two most promising distinct candidates as #1 and #2. Keep a third if someone makes a convincing case for a different angle. A new or combined candidate can replace one already on the shortlist. If the team disagrees about the order, state both reasons and carry the disputed candidate into the assessment step. The ranking is provisional, not a final vote.
3. **Make each concept clear:** For each shortlisted candidate, complete one sentence: **“For [user], [idea] helps achieve [outcome] by [main approach].”** A quick example can clarify an ambiguous phrase. Record these sentences in [section 2 of the day-of guide](WORKFLOW_TEMPLATE.md#2-brainstorm-and-turn-ideas-into-concepts). Do not design the demo or settle data and feasibility questions yet. Keep the other groups on the wall.

## Test the concepts and choose the proposed KBC product concept

Now test the clear concepts against the actual brief and judging criteria. Judge the complete product they could become, while checking that one meaningful part could be proved during the hackathon. Ask these questions aloud:

- **Mathieu — product and value:** Does the full product address the brief's user pain? What would change for the customer and KBC?
- **Diana — future experience:** How would a user actually use the product from need to outcome? Where would they consent, review, or act?
- **Damiens — dependencies and proof:** What data and integrations would the real product need? Can one important mechanism be shown with permitted inputs in four hours?

Cross out an idea that fails a mandatory rule or needs data the team cannot use. Mark an answer unknown when it cannot be checked quickly. Do not spend these minutes on an architecture or a feature list.

Compare the remaining concepts on customer value, distinctive mechanism, KBC fit, and whether one important part can be proved. Each person may defend or revise their preference after hearing the challenges. The team chooses together based on the brief and this discussion, not the provisional order. Mathieu records the reason, the strongest unresolved assumption, and who will check it. Together, state the **proposed future KBC product's** user, complete intended experience, distinctive mechanism, customer and KBC value, and main dependency in [section 3](WORKFLOW_TEMPLATE.md#3-choose-the-kbc-product-concept-and-outline-its-vision). If a material unknown could invalidate the concept, assign an immediate check and keep the next best concept as a fallback. Then identify one small journey or mechanism to prove in the four-hour prototype. Once that proof is clear, move to the interface discussion; the team need not finish every worksheet field first.

**Why this format:** The silent start gives everyone an initial voice; the open brainstorm allows the team to build on one another's ideas. Experiments found that waiting to speak can suppress idea generation, so keep contributions brief. Interviews with hackathon winners found both brainstorming and individual writing followed by group selection. Those studies support the ingredients; they do not establish ideal durations or a required moment to start coding. See [Nijstad et al.](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0022103103000404) and [Gama et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2206.04744v1).
